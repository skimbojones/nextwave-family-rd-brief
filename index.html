Where the R&D is going: NextOS Family

NextOS is Nextwave's AI operating system for small business. Family is its second track: the same bench of AI seats, pointed at the household instead of the office. It is built and tested on one real family, mine, every morning. Everything below is the live product as of this week, not a mockup. Names, faces and schools are blurred out.

The pitch in four sentences

We have built an AI bench that runs the admin layer of a household: it reads both school inboxes every morning, separates the three things that actually need a parent from the forty that do not, and puts the week for four people on one screen. It also runs the children's learning: a short daily quiz mapped to their year level, illustrated and read aloud, that picks up what a teacher flags and has it in the next morning's questions. Customer zero is my own family, which is why it gets used at 7am every day rather than demoed once a quarter. It is the same chassis as the commercial track, so it is built once and licensable to any household.

1 · The family screen

One page, four people, the week ahead. Each child shows what is on today, what is on this weekend, and whether anything from school needs a parent. The two adults sit underneath. This is the screen the household opens on a phone at breakfast.

The week at a glance: two children, two parents, and a live count of what school needs from you.

2 · The whole family, one wall

The same week, opened out. Chores rotate automatically between the two children by week number, so nobody argues about whose turn it is. The button at the top right is voice: press it and ask the household assistant what you have forgotten.

Four people, one week, and a voice button for the question every parent asks on a Sunday night.

3 · The school sweep

Two children at two different schools means two newsletter streams, two parent portals and a steady drip of notices. The bench sweeps both every morning at about half past five, reads everything, and sorts it into what needs a decision and what is just news. This week it read nine messages and flagged five as needing a parent.

The School Board, swept daily. Nine messages read, five need you, nothing slipped.

4 · Per child, sorted into buckets

The same school signal, split per child into School, Sport, Health and Activities. Each bucket carries a real count and an honest empty state: when there is nothing, it says so rather than inventing filler. A parent can see in three seconds which child is the one that needs attention today.

One card per child, four buckets each, real counts and honest empty states.

The same board condensed: what is open per child, and the week underneath.

5 · The children have their own page

Each child gets a page they open themselves. Their week, the chore they are on, their pocket money balance, and two buttons: the morning quiz and this week's magazine. It is deliberately built for a child to run without a parent driving it.

A child's own page: week, chore, savings, quiz, magazine. No parent required to operate it.

The same child's room with a short brief written for them by the assistant: one thing needs you this week, plus three to glance at.

6 · The morning quiz

Six questions before school. They are not generic: they are mapped to the child's actual year level in the Australian curriculum, and to the specific gaps their teacher has named. There are eleven different question shapes, so a week does not feel like the same worksheet five times.

Six quick ones, a star at the end. Two children, two year levels, two different sets.

Every question gets a picture

Each question is illustrated and read aloud in a real voice. There are fifty-seven question illustrations in the library so far, generated in-house, with a scene ledger that stops the same picture coming round twice in a fortnight.

An illustrated card. A picture for every question, a different mix every day.

Whose turn: both children, their star counts, and one tap into the day's set.

7 · The loop that makes it worth building

This is the part no calendar app does. The teacher flags a spelling problem through the school portal. The bench reads it, works out what it means, and puts one card a day from that teacher's own word list into the child's quiz the next morning. The parent sees a card like the one below and taps once to approve.

School signal in, learning out: a teacher's note becomes tomorrow's quiz card, with a one-tap approval.

8 · It talks

The assistant runs on voice as well as screen, on the same phone the family already carries. This matters more in a house than in an office: nobody is going to sit down at a laptop to ask whether the library books are due.

The household assistant, live on voice.

Why this is an asset, not a burn

  • It is the same platform, pointed somewhere else. The bench, the voice layer, the decision cards, the hosting and the app shell were all built and paid for by the commercial track. Family reuses them. What is genuinely new here is the school sweep and the learning engine.
  • No new paid feeds. The real estate track carries data subscriptions. This one does not: it reads the school communications the family already receives. Marginal cost for a second household is hosting and AI compute, not development.
  • The category is real, but it is split in three. Cozi sells the shared family calendar. Compass Education sells schools the parent portal. 3P Learning, an Australian listed company, sells Mathletics and Reading Eggs to parents and schools. Three separate purchases, three separate logins, and none of them joins the school signal to the child's practice. That join is the product.
  • Honest run cost. Hosting and AI compute, monthly, cancellable, nothing locked in. If we stopped tomorrow the spend stops with it.

The proof gate and the next 90 days

There is one measurement that counts, and it is not ours. One child's school runs a cumulative spelling review, seventy-five items, once a term. Term 1 was 42 out of 75. End of Term 2 was 57 out of 75, which is 56% up to 76%. The morning quiz was running for the whole of that gain.

What we will not claim: that the quiz caused it. There is no control, the child has a good teacher, and a term is a term. The claim that survives scrutiny is that it was running the whole time she improved, and that is enough to keep going on.

  • The Term 3 review, same seventy-five item instrument, same teacher. Using the school's own metric rather than one we invented is the entire point. That is the gate.
  • Then package it: a per-household subscription, with the school sweep and the learning loop as the reason to buy rather than the calendar.
  • Then, and only then, the invest-further and pricing decision, made on evidence.
